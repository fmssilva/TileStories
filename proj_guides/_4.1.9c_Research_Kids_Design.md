# TileStories: research for the Playful (kids) look

Scope: who the playful look is for, what kids' and teen museum and AR products do well, a "hand-drawn but premium" visual language built from azulejo heritage, and how much UI and motion is acceptable over a camera view of the *Grande Panorama de Lisboa*. Sources are linked inline. Everything is paraphrased. Points marked *(observation)* are design-practice notes with no linked source.

The artwork in brief: the panel dates from about 1700 and is attributed to Gabriel del Barco. It is blue-on-white faience measuring 114 × 2,247 cm and shows roughly 14 km of the Tagus waterfront from Algés to Xabregas. It includes the castle, São Vicente, the Ribeira Palace, Jerónimos, Belém, the Ribeira market, coaches, sedan chairs and a tide mill. The convent that now houses the museum appears at the far right ([Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/grand-panorama-of-lisbon-ribeira-royal-palace/zAEzUKmNN9GdGg); [Museu Nacional do Azulejo](https://www.museunacionaldoazulejo.pt/en/vista-lisboa)). That everyday-life detail is the best raw material for kids' content: they hunt for small things.

## 1. Target age and audience split

**Children are very sensitive to age.** NN/g recommends designing for at least three bands (3–5, 6–8 and 9–12) and found that children react badly to content even one grade above or below their own. In their tests a 6-year-old called a site "for babies" ([NN/g, children's sites](https://nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues)). Smashing's practical guide also recommends aiming at a two-year core range rather than "kids" in general ([Smashing](https://smashingmagazine.com/2024/02/practical-guide-design-children)).

**Reading.** Younger children read hesitantly, older children scan, and all of them skip instructions ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues)). Text for 6–8-year-olds should be large (about 18–19 px), and every action needs feedback ([Smashing](https://smashingmagazine.com/2024/02/practical-guide-design-children)). A playful look aimed at around 9 can use short text, but every story needs audio narration and icon or picture support so 7-year-olds can still take part.

**Phone ownership: the phone is usually a parent's.** In the US, 19% of 8-year-olds, 53% of 11-year-olds and 69% of 12-year-olds had their own smartphone ([Common Sense, 2019](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/articles/tweens-teens-and-phones-what-our-2019-research-reveals)). In the UK, Ofcom (2026) reports 56% at age 10 and 83% at age 11, with the move to secondary school as the tipping point ([Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/younger-phone-owners-the-rise-of-ai-and-consumption-over-creation-our-latest-look-at-uk-childrens-media-lives)). For 7–10-year-olds, assume co-use: an adult's phone that is handed over or shared. Onboarding must never say "your phone" or assume the person tapping is the person playing.

**Family visits run on conversation.** In family museum visits, adults read aloud, explain and ask questions, while children point things out and often set the route. Short text that both adults and early readers can manage, plus collaborative activities, keeps families on the topic ([Massarani et al., Frontiers in Education, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.669467/full)). The Rijksmuseum's Family Quest is aimed at ages 7+ (a reviewer suggests up to about 12). It needs at least two players and gives each player different questions so they cooperate ([review](https://www.mummytravels.com/?p=60168)). The National Gallery's AR trail *The Keeper of Paintings* was co-designed with more than 80 children and targets ages 7–11 ([Royal Holloway](https://es.royalholloway.ac.uk/about-us/news/children-help-create-a-magical-augmented-reality-family-trail-for-the-national-gallery)). The sector's own benchmark for playful AR at a painting is therefore **7–11**.

**Teens reject "kid".** NN/g's teen research found that the word "kid" drives teens away, and that childish visuals, bright loud colour schemes and gratuitous animation put them off. Neutral, mature visuals worked better, and interactive features such as quizzes and games were welcome when they let teens actually do something ([NN/g, teens](https://nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers)). Museum practitioners make the same point: teens want to be treated as young adults, "not eight-year-olds" ([inkl/Guardian](https://www.inkl.com/news/why-have-museums-forgotten-the-teens)).

**Recommendation**
- **Design the playful look for 9-year-olds (core 8–10). It should stretch down to 7 with audio and adult help, and up to 11.** That matches the Keeper of Paintings and Rijksmuseum precedents and the age range where co-use is still the norm.
- **Move the cut-off from 12/13 to 12.** Twelve-year-olds are at secondary school, most own phones, and they are the group most likely to call the playful look babyish. Sending them to Premium costs little. Showing them stickers and the word "kids" costs a lot.
- **Teens and adults (12+) get Premium**, not a third look for now (see §3).

**Onboarding labels.** "Who's exploring?" has three problems: it assumes one explorer, it doesn't name the dimension, and it makes the person holding the phone answer for someone else. "Kids · 8–12" uses the word teens reject and puts 12-year-olds in the wrong group. Suggested wording:

> **Who is this tour for?**
> ◉ **Ages 7–11**: playful adventure with games, stickers and narration
> ◉ **Ages 12+**: the full museum tour, calm and detailed
> *Exploring together? Pick the youngest. You can switch at any time in Settings.*

This names the dimension (who the tour is for), leads with an unambiguous number, avoids "kids" and "children", works whoever holds the phone, and handles mixed families. Alternative heading: "Choose your tour". Avoid "Child/Adult" and "Fun/Serious", because they imply the Premium tour isn't fun.

## 2. Principles and patterns from kids' and teen museum, AR and learning products

| Product | What to borrow | How it keeps attention on the real object |
|---|---|---|
| **Keeper of Paintings** (National Gallery) | A magical-guardian story, puzzles and a collection of "gems" tied to specific paintings ([RHUL](https://es.royalholloway.ac.uk/about-us/news/children-help-create-a-magical-augmented-reality-family-trail-for-the-national-gallery)) | Every reward is unlocked *at* a painting |
| **Rijksmuseum Family Quest** | 8 varied challenges, about 1 hour, different questions per player, an anagram letter per stage leading to a small prize ([review](https://www.mummytravels.com/?p=60168)) | Players have to talk about details in the painting |
| **Smithsonian *Skin and Bones*** | Point the camera at a specimen and AR shows how it moved, plus short videos and mini-games such as identifying bat calls ([Smithsonian](https://newsdesk.si.edu/releases/smithsonian-brings-historic-specimens-life-free-skin-and-bones-mobile-app)) | Praised for encouraging visitors to put the device down and look harder ([Blooloop](https://blooloop.com/museum/opinion/ar-experience-checklist-museums/)) |
| **National Museum of Scotland Explorer** | Choose a theme ("fiercest", "smelliest"), follow clues, find the object, unlock a photo badge ([The List](https://list.co.uk/news/19188/museum-explorer-iphone-app)) | You only get the badge by finding the real object |
| **Duolingo** | Few, rounded, simple shapes with "the fewest details needed", bright colour, white space ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/shape-language-duolingos-art-style/)). Characters lip-sync with real-time animation (Rive) and react to right and wrong answers ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/world-character-visemes)) | Detail is stripped so it doesn't compete with the lesson |
| **Khan Academy Kids** | One main guide (Kodi) gives instructions, tips and *specific* praise ("You traced the letter D"). Kids earn items for the characters ([Khan Kids](https://khankids.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360049358751-Learn-more-about-the-characters-inside-Khan-Academy-Kids)) | The guide points at the task rather than performing |
| **Toca Boca** | "We organize freedom": open-ended digital toys with no points, timers or failure states ([Kidscreen](https://kidscreen.com/?p=49360)) | Kids explore and are never punished |
| **Monument Valley** | Every screen is designed to be worth hanging on a wall. It is made for non-gamers, completable and not punishing ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/designing-the-surprise-mobile-game-hit-i-monument-valley-i-)) | The art carries the experience, and the UI stays almost invisible |
| **LEGO / UNICEF RITEC** | Eight well-being dimensions (autonomy, competence, creativity, relationships, emotions, identity, inclusion, safety). Test with real children and put well-being before stickiness ([Cooney Center](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/2024/11/19/into-the-digital-future-shuli-gilutz/); [UNICEF](https://www.unicef.org/designing-digital-play-childrens-well-being-mind)) | No dark patterns |
| **Pokémon GO** *(observation)* | A collection log (Pokédex), medals, and an AR camera players can switch off | The collection is the long-term hook and AR is optional, which supports a non-AR fallback |

**What makes them work, and how they balance fun against focus**
1. **Rewards are earned by looking at the real thing.** Gems, badges and anagram letters are all tied to a specific object. For TileStories, a sticker should only unlock after the child finds or identifies something on the wall.
2. **Short, varied tasks.** Rijksmuseum uses 8 different challenge types in about 1 hour. Mix "find it", "count it", "spot the difference", "listen" and "what happened here?".
3. **Cooperative and family-friendly.** Give split roles ("you find, I read"), use prompts adults can read aloud, and offer questions that make families talk.
4. **One guide, used sparingly.** A single character gives instructions, encouragement and specific praise, then gets out of the way. Kodi works this way, and Duolingo characters react and then go quiet.
5. **Collections over points.** Stamps, stickers and an album appeal to collecting instincts. Badges can raise engagement without harming intrinsic motivation, provided they mark real learning ([Do Badges Increase Engagement?, SIGITE 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10290885-do-badges-increase-student-engagement-motivation)). Smashing warns that relying on extrinsic rewards can weaken long-term motivation ([Smashing](https://smashingmagazine.com/2024/02/practical-guide-design-children)). Don't use streaks, timers, leaderboards between strangers, or loss-aversion mechanics.
6. **No failure states.** Wrong answers should get a gentle hint and a retry, as in Toca Boca and Monument Valley. Feedback should follow every action ([Smashing](https://smashingmagazine.com/2024/02/practical-guide-design-children)).
7. **AR only where it reveals something.** Ask whether the AR shows something only AR can show ([Blooloop](https://blooloop.com/museum/opinion/ar-experience-checklist-museums/)). On the panorama, examples are the Ribeira Palace that the earthquake destroyed, the river traffic, and the tide mill turning.
8. **Sound does a lot of the work.** Kids expect sound and animation, while adults usually dislike them ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/childrens-websites-usability-issues)). Use narration for every card, short audio cues for found, correct and collected, and haptics on success (Apple recommends both, [HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/augmented-reality.md)). Gallery etiquette means sound must work on headphones and the app must stay usable when muted, with captions.
9. **Audio keeps eyes up.** Audio-first guides let visitors keep looking at the artwork while they listen ([Walkie Talkie / ISPR](https://ispr.info/?p=36745)). Narrated stories should play while the camera stays on the wall.
10. **Co-design and testing with children** was standard practice for the Keeper of Paintings and RITEC.

## 3. Visual language: "hand-drawn but premium"

**Make the heritage the illustration system.** The panel is blue-and-white Baroque azulejo, a tradition Gabriel del Barco helped bring to Portugal under Delft influence. The wider azulejo vocabulary includes carpet patterns (*tapete*), friezes and borders, *albarradas* (vases flanked by birds or dolphins), Rococo shells, the armillary sphere, and the *figuras de convite*: life-size cut-out "invitation figures" that greet visitors, a form unique to Portugal ([Wikipedia: Azulejo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azulejo)). This gives TileStories a ready-made, culturally authentic doodle kit.

- **Line.** All playful illustration is drawn in cobalt brush line, the colour of the panel itself, with slight wobble and varied weight. Use one line colour family (cobalt #1F3F9A to #0E2466) on warm tin-glaze white (#F7F2E7). Keep accent colours minimal: tile yellow/ochre (as in historic border tiles) and one warm coral for "go" actions. This keeps the look cultural rather than generic candy colours.
- **The guide is a figura de convite.** A cut-out tile character, such as a little halberdier or a lady in a tricorn, painted in azulejo style. It welcomes you at onboarding, gives hints and reacts. The character is historically grounded, unique to Portugal, and the cut-out silhouette suits AR. One guide only.
- **Background textures.** Use a faint 14 cm tile grid with crazing (fine cracks), plus patterned-tile and frieze strips as panel headers. Animal and object doodles come from the panel itself: carracks, gulls, dolphins, sedan chairs, coaches, the windmill.
- **Wobble with discipline.** Pre-render 3 to 4 "boiling line" variants for celebrations only. Static UI uses a single fixed hand-drawn outline: shapes should feel drawn, not jitter. Premium quality comes from consistent stroke weight, a tight palette and generous white space (the Duolingo rule of fewest details).

**Component treatments (Playful)**

| Component | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Buttons** | Pill or rounded-rect shape with a hand-drawn cobalt outline (2–3 px) and a solid offset shadow of 3–4 px down-right in deep cobalt. On press, the button moves into the shadow (translate, shadow goes to 0) with a haptic tick. Primary is coral fill, secondary is tile-white. Minimum target 48 px. |
| **Panels / sheets** | Paper-white card with a tile-grid watermark and a frieze-strip header. Top corners use a hand-cut edge or a slight 0.5–1° tilt, never on body text. Sheet heights are a peek (about 25%), half and full. In AR, default to the peek. |
| **Cards (story / POI)** | "Tile" cards: square-ish with a small patterned-tile corner badge, a cropped detail from the real panel as the hero image (outlined, sticker-style), a title in a rounded display face, and an audio button first. |
| **Chips / tags** | Small sticker shapes with a white outline and soft drop shadow, slightly rotated, using icons from the panel (castle, church, ship). |
| **Nav** | 3 to 4 tabs maximum (Wall, Album, Games, Me) in a floating bar with a hand-drawn outline. The active tab gets a cobalt doodle underline. Hide the bar in AR focus mode. |
| **Markers (POIs in AR)** | A small cobalt-ringed tile disc with a pictogram, a gentle idle "breathe" (about 2 s cycle, scale 1.0 to 1.06), and one-time ripple sparkles when the marker comes into view. Found POIs flip to show a stamp. Keep labels in screen space and facing the user (Apple HIG). |
| **Onboarding** | 3 screens maximum. The figura de convite steps out of a tile and greets the visitor, then a "point at the big blue wall" coach with an illustrated phone outline, then the age question above. Narrated, with Skip always visible. |
| **Collections** | A sticker album styled as a blank tile panel. Each found detail fills one tile, and completing a row or frieze reveals a mini-story. This is a collection, not points. |
| **Celebrations** | 1 to 1.5 s: the tile flips or "fires" in a kiln-glow, cobalt confetti shaped like tile fragments falls, the guide reacts, and a haptic plays. Then it returns to the wall automatically. No full-screen takeovers mid-AR (Google AR guidance), except at the end of a quest. |
| **Typography** | A rounded, friendly display face for headings (for example Baloo 2, Fredoka or Nunito Black) and Atkinson Hyperlegible for body, kept from Premium for continuity and legibility. Body at least 18 px. |
| **3D / parallax** | Use them on non-AR screens only: the onboarding diorama, album and story cards. Layered cut-out tiles (paper-theatre style) with gentle gyroscope parallax of 4–8 px. |

**Teens: Premium, a third look, or a variant?** Evidence says teens want neutral, mature visuals and dislike bright, loud, animated interfaces, but they like doing things (quizzes, games) ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers)). **Recommend Premium for 12+, with a "challenge" layer rather than a third look.** That means timed-free quizzes, a shareable result card designed like a bold tile poster (social-native, made for screenshots), and the same azulejo line art used as editorial illustration. A third "bold/social" theme triples QA and art cost for a small in-museum audience. Revisit only if testing shows 12–15s disengaging with Premium.

## 4. Attention budget over the camera view

**Guidelines**
- Apple: give the camera the whole screen, avoid clutter, keep text in the scene to a minimum, put needed text and controls in screen space, consider translucency for persistent controls, prefer 3D hints over text overlays, keep sessions short with breaks, and delay hiding content for about 1 s when the image is lost to avoid flicker ([Apple HIG, AR](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/augmented-reality.md)).
- Google: interrupt the AR scene as little as possible, keep controls few so they can be used almost without looking, and avoid pop-ups or full-screen takeovers unless the user asks for them ([ARCore UI](https://developers.google.com/ar/design/interaction/ui)). Long sessions cause fatigue, so build in stopping points and reminders to look around ([ARCore safety](https://developers.google.com/ar/design/user/safety-comfort)).
- **Split attention.** Information shown away from the object it describes forces learners to integrate two sources mentally and raises cognitive load. AR that overlays information *on* the object can reduce that cost. This was tested with university students and is being replicated with elementary children ([Altmeyer et al., DFKI, 2021](https://dfki.de/web/forschung/projekte-publikationen/publikation/11870)). The implication is to anchor content to the tile region it explains rather than opening detached cards.
- **Heads-down risk.** Museum technologists argue the bottleneck is attention, not information, and that screens pull eyes away from art ([ISPR / Walkie Talkie](https://ispr.info/?p=36745)).
- **Motion accessibility.** Parallax and interaction-triggered motion can cause dizziness and nausea in people with vestibular disorders. Provide a way to turn it off and respect the operating system's reduce-motion setting ([W3C, WCAG 2.3.3](https://w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/animation-from-interactions)).

**A proposed budget for TileStories (Playful, AR view)**
- **At least 75–80% of the screen is the live wall.** Persistent chrome is a top bar of about 44 px (back, sound, help) and one bottom control or tray peek of about 25%. Everything else is anchored to the wall.
- **At most 3–5 markers visible** at once (the existing "pointers at most" setting fits this), plus one off-screen arrow cue at the edge.
- **One moving thing at a time.** Idle "breathe" on markers and nothing else. Celebrations last no more than 1.5 s and run only after an action. No looping background animation in AR.
- **No decorative doodles over the camera.** Wobble, stickers and offset shadows belong on sheets, cards and non-AR screens. Over the wall, use only cobalt rings and pictograms that look like part of the tile.
- **Look up, then look down.** Every task starts with a prompt to look at the wall ("Find the ship with three sails") and only then opens content. Audio plays while the camera stays up.
- **Session pacing.** Quests of 5–8 minutes with a natural "rest your arms" break, plus an AR-off mode (a photographed panel you can pan) for tired arms, crowded rooms or devices without AR.
- **Reduce-motion mode** removes parallax and confetti, and turns tile flips into a cross-fade.

## Summary (15 lines)

1. **Target age:** design the Playful look for 9-year-olds (core 8–10), stretching to 7 with narration and adult help and up to 11. Send ages 12+ to Premium.
2. **Labels:** "Who is this tour for?" with **"Ages 7–11 · playful adventure"** and **"Ages 12+ · full museum tour"**, plus the note "Exploring together? Pick the youngest. Switch anytime." Never use "kids", and never assume who owns the phone.
3. **Principle: tie rewards to looking.** Stickers and stamps unlock only by finding or identifying something on the real wall.
4. **Principle: collections, not points.** A tile-panel sticker album that fills tile by tile, with no streaks, timers or leaderboards.
5. **Principle: one guide.** A *figura de convite* tile character who welcomes, hints and gives specific praise, then gets out of the way.
6. **Principle: heritage is the illustration source.** Cobalt brush line on tin-glaze white, patterned-tile and frieze textures, and doodles taken from the panel itself.
7. **Principle: short, varied, cooperative tasks** in 5–8-minute quests that families can read aloud and talk about.
8. **Principle: no failure.** Gentle hints, retry, and feedback on every tap.
9. **Principle: audio-first.** Narrate every card, use short sound cues and haptics, work fully when muted, and keep eyes on the wall while listening.
10. **Principle: anchor content to the tile it explains** to reduce split attention, and keep at least 75% of the screen as the live wall.
11. **Principle: one motion at a time in AR.** Markers breathe, celebrations last 1.5 s or less, and parallax and 3D stay on non-AR screens. Respect reduce-motion.
12. **Principle: tactile, premium components.** Hand-drawn outlines, offset-shadow buttons that press in, sticker chips, and tile cards using real panel crops. Disciplined palette and stroke.
13. **Avoid:** the word "kids", babyish candy colours, constant wobble or jitter, decorative doodles over the camera feed, and full-screen takeovers mid-AR.
14. **Avoid:** streaks, loss-aversion and dark patterns, multiple mascots, long text, and AR effects that don't reveal anything about the panel.
15. **Teens:** use Premium plus a challenge layer (quizzes and a shareable tile-poster result card), not a third look. Revisit after testing with 12–15-year-olds.
